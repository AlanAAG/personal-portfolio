How WeChat turned everyday conversations into a digital ecosystem across China

Weixin currently has more than 1.4 billion monthly active accounts and its Mini Programs facilitate more than RMB 8 trillion in transactions. When I moved to China, I was mesmerized by Weixin, the “everything app.” People used it to chat, pay, follow creators and access everyday services.

How could one company change so many habits across a country? I dug into its business model and found out how Tencent actually managed to do it.

Make the next step easier

Weixin launched as a communication app in 2011. Pay followed in 2013 and Mini Programs in 2017. The order matters: people came to talk, then merchants could offer a next step when a conversation became a plan.

Without that repeated use case, a startup’s “super-app” is just a bundle of features with no reason for people to return.

Make the next step easier

Imagine someone sharing a sponsored restaurant video in a Weixin group chat. Friends discuss it, open the restaurant’s Mini Program and pay a deposit through Weixin Pay. This is one possible journey, not how most bookings necessarily happen.

Creators, developers and merchants make the content, build services and serve the meal. Tencent provides the social network, discovery tools and payment system that connect them.

Mini Programs are useful for occasional services: nobody wants a new app just to book one class. For merchants, lightweight storefronts and QR payments can digitize parts of discovery, ordering and payment. They still have to keep information accurate, deliver the service and handle complaints.

Weixin did not just put businesses on a screen. It changed the path from a recommendation to a real service, using shared infrastructure.

Build a platform, not every business on it

The variety comes from merchants, creators and developers. Tencent reported 500 million monthly Mini Games players and 500,000 active developers in 2026. More than half of players find those games through Weixin’s existing features. That shows the value of building where people already spend time, though it does not guarantee developer income.

The loop is powerful: useful services give people more reasons to stay, and a large audience gives businesses more reasons to join. A competitor can copy a storefront or payment link. Rebuilding the contacts, habits, developers, merchants and payment acceptance together is harder.

Basic communication is generally free. Tencent earns from ads, merchant payment fees, qualifying Mini Shops transactions and other platform services. It does not publish Weixin-only profit, and high transaction volume does not prove that merchants profit after their costs.

The advantage is not automatic

Weixin can be the place where friends discuss dinner without being the place where they book it. Meituan specializes in local services and says its AI assistant can help find and reserve a restaurant. Douyin can win attention, while Alipay can handle a payment. These rivals do not need to replace Weixin’s social network to win one step of the journey.

That is why I would judge any new Weixin feature by the completed customer job, not the number of features added. Did the group actually book? Was it easier? Did the merchant earn more after costs? If the answer is no, the ecosystem is impressive but the new service has not created much value.

What I would take to another market

My biggest learning is not to export the Chinese super-app as a template. In a market with weaker connectivity, lower smartphone access or less digital payment use, forcing every step into one app could make the experience worse. The World Bank found that 42% of adults in low- and middle-income countries made a digital merchant payment in 2024. That is growing, but it is far from universal.

I would start by finding a behavior people already trust, perhaps a messaging group or a local merchant relationship. Then I would map one unfinished journey, such as arranging a class or confirming a service appointment. Where does the customer have to repeat information, switch channels or wait for confirmation? A small product could remove just one of those handoffs.

The first version should work with local realities: simple merchant tools, low data use, a payment method people already use and a human or cash fallback where needed. I would pilot it with a few merchants and measure completed services, customer effort, merchant profit and trust. Only if those improve would I add more services.

Weixin taught me that a digital ecosystem is not built by collecting features. It grows when each new connection makes a real task easier for users and worthwhile for the people who serve them.
